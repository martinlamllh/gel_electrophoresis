1. Agarose Gel Concentration

Agarose gel is not a uniform solid; it is a porous matrix acting like a microscopic sieve. By altering the concentration of agarose powder dissolved in the buffer, we can control the structure of this sieve.

  • Lower Concentration (e.g. 0.5%): Creates a loose matrix with large pores. Ideal for separating very large DNA fragments, as they can navigate the loose mesh.
  • Higher Concentration (e.g. 2.0%): Creates a dense, tight matrix with very small pores. Essential for separating small DNA fragments, providing enough friction to resolve slight size differences.

Use the slider to see how increasing the concentration dynamically shrinks the pore sizes in the microscopic network.

2. Electrical Current & DNA

Gel electrophoresis uses an electrical field to push molecules through the gel matrix. The direction of movement is dictated entirely by the inherent electrical charge of the molecules.

The Motive Force: Repulsion and Attraction

DNA is fundamentally a negatively charged molecule. When placed in the electric field, it is repelled by the negative Cathode (-) and strongly attracted towards the positive Anode (+). This creates a unidirectional flow from one end of the tank to the other.

3. Behaviour & Separation

Because the charge-to-mass ratio is uniform across all DNA molecules, separation in the gel is driven entirely by size (length). The agarose mesh acts as a physical obstacle course.

  • Long Fragments: Get entangled frequently in the matrix pores, increasing friction. They move slowly and stay near the top.
  • Short Fragments: Compact enough to easily weave through the microscopic pores. They encounter less resistance, migrate much faster, and travel further down the gel (but must be stopped before they run out!).

Molecular Source of Negative Charge

The uniform negative charge stems from the phosphate group (PO₄³⁻) in the backbone of every nucleotide.

4. SYBR Green Visualisation

Because DNA is inherently colourless and transparent, separating the fragments is useless unless we can see them. We achieve this using a fluorescent stain called SYBR Green.

Intercalation (Sim A)

SYBR Green possesses a flat, planar chemical structure. This shape allows it to slip completely between the stacked nitrogenous base pairs of the DNA double helix—a process known as intercalation.

Ultraviolet (UV) Activation (Sim B)

  • Free-floating dye molecules in the gel do not fluoresce significantly.
  • When intercalated into the rigid structure of DNA, the dye's molecular constraints change.
  • Upon exposure to UV light, the bound dye absorbs the high-energy light and strongly emits a bright green fluorescence, illuminating the exact location of the separated DNA bands.
